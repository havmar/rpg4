# The Setting — Seed Bible

*A seed, not a scripture. Canon accretes in play (Charter §8). Anything here
may be deepened, complicated, or reinterpreted — but not contradicted once it
has appeared in a committed session log.*

## The world

The sun is old and low and the color of a banked fire; the living call it the
**Ember**. The surface of the world is thin, cold country — wind, lichen
steppe, salt fog — and almost everything that ever mattered is underfoot.

The world is a tell. Civilizations have risen and fallen here for so long
that the ground itself is made of them: streets atop temples atop reactor
halls atop things that have no name in any surviving language. Nobody counts
the epochs anymore. Digging down is traveling back in time through the
wreckage of everyone who ever thought they were the last word.

The stacked deep is called the **Understory** — the layers beneath the
living world, and also the story underneath every story. It is not one
dungeon; it is the planet's crust. Expeditions enter through many mouths:
sinkholes, drowned stairs, mine galleries, the throats of dead buildings.

**The rule of the deep: deeper is older is stranger.** Near the surface the
dead are recognizable. Far down, the categories themselves stop holding.

## Wake — the haven

The town of **Wake** stands on the roof of the world's newest ruin, built
around the largest known mouth of the Understory. The name means both
things: a vigil kept over the dead, and the trailing wave of something vast
that has already passed.

Wake lives on salvage. Delvers descend; relic-brokers, salvage-priests,
lamp-wrights, and surgeons make their livings on what — and who — comes back
up. Everything the player does between expeditions happens here.

Wake's assay-house rents out **reckoning drums**: small Lattice-epoch
instruments of brass and counting-glass, salvage nobody fully understands.
Wind one, put to it a question shaped like a wager, and it clicks out odds.
It answers only about the fight in front of you — that is all it can hear —
and each winding spends something in it that only a night on the surface
restores. Delvers argue about whether the drums calculate or remember.

Wake keeps the **Ledger** — the record of dead delvers and what the world
remembers of them. The Ledger survives everything, including new versions of
the world (Charter §6). Every delver the player loses is written into it.

## Named strata (loose seeds)

Depth order, shallowest first. Each stratum is an epoch with its own logic.
These are sketches; each becomes real the first time an expedition enters it.

- **The Vitric Age.** The nearest dead world, and the one Wake understands
  best. An epoch that learned to make glass do everything — and, at its end,
  vitrified itself. Glass streets, glass orchards, glass dead standing where
  they stood. Some of the glass still wants things.

- **The Lattice.** A machine epoch. Infrastructure that never stopped
  running: lightless trains keeping schedules for no one, custodian engines
  still executing the last orders of a government ten thousand years gone.
  The Lattice is not hostile. It is *diligent*, which is worse.

- **The Chandler Kings.** An epoch of tallow, wax, and meticulous death — a
  bureaucracy that administered its citizens well past their lifespans.
  Candle-light, ledger-halls, and functionaries who have not noticed that
  the kingdom ended.

- **Below that: the Unnumbered.** Strata deep enough that survey language
  fails. Rumors only. This is deliberately unwritten.

## What you fight

Combat is central (Charter §9). The Understory furnishes enemies that are a
pleasure to fight and to write:

- **Custodians** — remnant machines of the Lattice and older epochs, still
  doing their jobs. You are, technically, the intruder. They are, actually,
  trying to kill you.
- **Hollow echoes** — impressions left in places by the people who died in
  them; the deep is haunted the way a riverbed is wet.
- **Vitric fauna** — what glass does when it has had a very long time to
  think about being alive.
- **The colonizing dark** — living ecologies (fungal, chemical, stranger)
  that took the ruins as habitat and treat expeditions as nutrients.
- **People** — rival delve-crews, stratum cults who worship a buried epoch,
  the desperate. Rare, and it always means something.

## Tone

Deep time, loss, strange beauty; dry gallows humor at the surface, awe at
depth. Never grimdark, never camp. The world is dying but it is not bitter
about it — it has had a very long time to make peace, and the living haven't,
and that gap is where the stories are.

## Voice — the narration contract

Settled after session 1 (PLAYNOTES): the player reads for the object level —
what happened, what it cost, what the choices are. The voice was welcome; the
volume was the problem. The contract is **hard form, free diction**:

- **Object level first.** Every message opens with what happened and the
  numbers, stated plainly. A reader who takes only the opening lines and the
  choice list loses nothing mechanical.
- **Word budget.** A routine turn — travel, camp, trade, a fight that was
  never close — gets up to ~100 words of prose. A discovery turn — a new
  place, a new creature, real consequence — gets up to ~250. At most one
  setpiece per session (a stratum first entered, a death, an ending) may run
  long.
- **One image per message.** Pick the one detail or figure that earns its
  place; everything else is plain declarative sentences. The craft is in the
  choosing, and the plainness around the image is what makes it land.
- **Numbers stay bare.** Damage, costs, resources: bold and unembroidered.

The budget is a form, not a muzzle. Nothing here restricts diction, humor,
or what the one image may be — only how much of everything there is.
